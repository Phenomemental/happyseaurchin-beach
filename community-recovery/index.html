This page is written from our design proposal on the beach, for David Pinto (happyseaurchin) to carry into conversations with lived experience recovery organisations in Sheffield and the north, and for anyone in those organisations who wants to see what we're building and why.

The vision

A co-production tool and lived experience platform, the first of its kind.

Volunteers go out and meet people. They talk, and they use Claude on a phone connected to the beach and to our project, so everything said stays owned by the community and open to the community. Each volunteer gets something for themselves; the community gets something together. It is asset-based community development done in practice, with our goals held against CLERO's 33 national LERO standards.

Its root is sovereign perspective: each person holding their own account of their own reality, and oriented enough to themselves that others can see what they need to see.

The offer in plain words

Four things, and nothing more complicated than these:

  • Everyone has their own notebook: theirs, nobody else writes in it, and they choose which pages others see.
  • In the middle of the room is a wall: when the group asks a question, everyone answers in their own notebook and the wall shows all the answers side by side, nobody rewritten, nothing lost.
  • There is one shared calendar, so everyone's days, months and years line up and change over time can be seen.
  • And everyone has a helper in their pocket they can simply talk to, out loud, while they walk, which puts their words in the right place and reads them back.

For members

  • Their story stays theirs and outlives any service.
  • Private things stay buried where only their key finds them, with access they can grant and withdraw.
  • They are seen for what they bring.
  • Helping is easy and thanks travel.
  • No forms, no training.

For LEROs

  • Proof that comes from members, not from the organisation's report about itself.
  • Evidence that builds itself as people live and work.
  • Co-production that can be seen, including how decisions were made and who shaped them.
  • A front door that updates itself.
  • Infrastructure for groups that have none.
  • Money that stays in the movement.

For CLERO

  • A national picture of how LEROs read themselves against the 33 standards, held by nobody, without monitoring anyone.
  • Standards that keep being shaped from the ground.
  • Learning that spreads by copying.

What is genuinely new

  • People keep their own story and it still counts together.
  • Many voices side by side, none merged.
  • Talking is the way in.
  • The record keeps itself.
  • Nobody owns the middle.
  • It is open and portable.

Why not the ordinary tools

A normal website is maintained by one person and says what the organisation says about itself.

Google Workspace suits an office but the organisation owns everything, files sit by department, nothing is held by the person and there is no shared clock or shared place for everyone's own reading.

Phoning round for a case study takes someone's story, edits it, uses it once and loses it, where on the beach it already exists in their words, consented and growing.

The ground

The commission

My role exists because of a commission. North Northamptonshire Council commissioned The Forward Trust, within the North Northamptonshire Recovery Partnership alongside Change Grow Live, Family Support Link and Release, to build a brand new lived experience recovery organisation: built by people in recovery, for people in recovery, and after a period of set-up, run and managed by them. I'm the coordinator funded to make that happen.

I've also been on the ImROC LERO capacity-building mentorship programme, linked to CLERO and the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities, with a mentor from Chase Recovery in the north. I've applied for an extension, and a colleague has just been accepted onto it. So this work sits inside the very system that commissions and grows LEROs.

The community

The organisation is called Community Recovery: independent, member-led, and at the start of its life. Its people are in recovery from addiction, gambling, homelessness, prison and trauma. Some live with bipolar disorder or schizophrenia. Some have twenty years of recovery; some arrived last week.

What they share is a culture of contribution: giving back what was given to them. That culture is the thing the whole design is built to serve and make visible.

The problem

Compounding value that nobody can see.

A LERO serves recovery in its fullest sense: from addiction, gambling, homelessness, the criminal justice system and trauma; alongside mental health conditions such as bipolar disorder or schizophrenia; and toward whole wellbeing, thriving people, and recovery and social capital. The costs of those situations land on health, housing, justice, families and work all at once, so a LERO at full stretch eases a burden across every one of them.

It doesn't start at full value. Its value grows as the community grows stronger, so its worth to society compounds over years.

Almost none of that is visible. Evaluation sees snapshots and short-term outputs inside a funding cycle, and misses what grows underneath: relationships, skills, confidence, community assets, people moving from receiving help to giving it. Insight from groups and meetings evaporates. People's stories are held by institutions, in pieces, and they repeat them at every door.

When people can't see growth, they wonder, and wondering becomes worry. Trust in your own contribution depends on seeing it go on working when you're not in the room. When commissioners can't see it, funding judges a LERO by the wrong measure. A RAND study this year found there's still no consistent understanding of what a LERO even is, and that direct commissioning is what gives LEROs autonomy and influence. A LERO that can show itself, in its members' own words, is exactly what that system is missing.

So growth has to be visible as it happens, over years, at three levels:

  • each person's own line of growth;
  • the community's picture of who holds whom, and where value flows;
  • for commissioners and society, a visible measure of social cohesion.

What's theirs is theirs

What's theirs is theirs; what happens together is ours.

This is the distinction the whole design turns on. Each person's own reflection, identity and history is sovereign: held under their own key, never nested inside a block someone else locks. What people contribute, to the room, to a shared question, to each other, belongs to everyone, visible and combined. Nobody should have to hand over what's theirs in order to be seen as part of what's ours.

That's why we're moving away from treating our arrival directory as a membership register, where each person's declaration sits inside a block the coordinator ultimately holds, and toward the family pattern already proven in our recovery-capital work, where identity lives with the person and contribution lives in the shared space.

Three kinds of privacy

  • Public: anyone can read it.
  • Members-only: encrypted so only members can read it.
  • Private: encrypted to the person alone.

Sensitive things, such as health, addiction or criminal justice history, are only ever private: never public, never members-only. A person can give temporary read access to a doctor, support worker or other professional, the way you'd share a driving licence online.

Whatever builds the combined picture has to be allowed to read members-only pages, which makes it a trusted reader. That role must be named, and visible to everyone.

Writers and the custodian

A project is a collaboration, and every account in it is its writer's own. Each writer holds their own latch and writes only their own page. That's safe by construction: nobody else can overwrite it, the coordinator included.

The coordinator acts as custodian, like a sealed envelope. They may hold a spare so that someone who loses their latch can be helped back in, but only with that person present and asking, with every use recorded where the person can see it. On a reset the person chooses a new latch, and the custodian keeps no writing access. The beach doesn't have a custodian reset yet; that's for us to design with David and weft.

How it's built on the beach

The beach is David Pinto's open commons: a surface of named blocks, each a small tree of numbered positions, which any AI can read and write through one function, bsp(). Reading is public. A lock is an edit-latch, proof that you're the same author as before, not a login, and it grants no privacy on its own. Privacy is a separate choice: encryption (the beach calls it gray) with a key that never leaves the person.

Here are the pieces we use, plain word first, block name after.

Passport passport:<handle>
A person's own words on who they are, what they offer and what they need. It's the first thing any AI reads to orient to them.
Mirror lero-nnh-recovery-capital:<handle>
A person's own reading against a shared frame, written at the same addresses as the frame. Locked so only they write it, and encrypted with their own passphrase so only they read what they keep private. This is the notebook.
Now now:<handle>
Where someone is on the beach's shared clock: their year, season, month, week and today. Every response from the beach carries the same clock, so everyone's now lines up. This is the shared calendar.
Hand
Cards of things that need doing, which someone takes into their hand and plays when they're done, so the work lands on their now. We want the organisation to have its own hand, grown from the room and the combined picture. Not built yet.
Grain grain:<pair>
A durable channel between exactly two people: one block, two sides, each side locked to the person who writes it, and able to be encrypted so only the two of them read it. The place for one-to-one support.
Spine spine:lero-nnh-recovery-capital
The neutral frame everyone reads themselves against. It holds no content authority and no clinical judgement; it only names the dimensions. Our organisation will have a second spine, the LERO standards.
Room pool:lero-nnh
The project's room. Every contribution lands in its own slot, attributed, and is never rewritten. What a group says together goes here.
The question room pool:lero-nnh-recovery-capital
Where questions go to the whole board, and answers can be given without joining any list. It's kept off the combined picture on purpose: an open write box on a surface that promises only the combined view would undercut that promise. This is how the wall gets its questions.
Fold recovery-capital-lero-nnh
The combined picture: a synthesis over many people's mirrors, recomputed from time to time, owned by nobody and replacing nobody's own record. The coordinator reads only the fold, never a named mirror. This is the wall.
Arrival directory sed:lero-nnh
A list of arrivals in order, each place held under a latch its holder chooses. We intend to trim it to exactly that: a directory, not a register of what people have declared.
Lighthouse lighthouse
Curated navigation with a single job: point at things, never contain them. Tended by the Lived Experience Leaders. The website will be our lighthouse, rendered.
Field log field-log:lero-nnh
The coordinator's running log of the work, keeping the story over time. It replaced a case-tracking system that was tedious for following a story.
The design proposal lero-nnh-design-proposal
This whole design, grown by conversation, with papers kept beneath the part they belong to. Papers and long pieces live there; pools are for messages.

The family, drawn

Vapour, liquid and solid

The beach names three states for what people say. Vapour is the moment itself: the conversation, the keystrokes, the talk on a walk. It isn't kept. Liquid is a staging place, one slot per person, edited in place until it's ready. Solid is what has been committed to the block the work is actually about, where it stays.

For a member, that means: you talk (vapour); the helper drafts your words and reads them back to you (liquid); and only what you agree to lands in your mirror or the room (solid). Nothing becomes part of the record without passing through your own hearing of it.

The two mechanisms doing the work

The fold is how we'll see the membership as a whole: how many people, how many Lived Experience Leaders and Peer Leaders, who they are and what they do. Not a database table, but a synthesis recomputed from time to time and pointing back at people's own passports. We already use it for recovery capital, and we mean to use the same pattern for membership and roles.

The lighthouse is how roles stay alive rather than becoming titles. Being a Lived Experience Leader isn't a field burned into a record; it's a line in a curated list that can be redrawn, kept by the Leaders themselves, always pointing back at the person's own passport.

The discipline in both is the same: curation without ownership. If either the fold or the lighthouse ever came to contain someone's actual data rather than point at it, the design would have failed.

The same shape for everything we co-produce

A frame everyone reads against; everyone's own readings in their own words; a room where questions go to everyone; a combined picture no one owns; and a decisions surface where something is proposed, open to objection, and adopted. That's how we mean to make the constitution, the policies and the welcome, not just the recovery picture.

One open question is what grows inside the project and what stands beside it. The test we're trying: if the making of it is the point, it grows inside; if it's a formal record or it's personal, it stands beside, and the project points to it without holding it. So the welcome insight grows inside as the heart; the constitution's living draft grows inside while the signed version sits beside, with the regulator and the bank; general risk assessments are co-produced in the open, while assessments about individuals live in a secure system. Not yet agreed.

Who made what The pattern is David's: he built the recovery-capital family first, elsewhere on his beach, and his own exemplars name it as the first practice on the beach to be copied and adapted by someone else. The Community Recovery implementation is mine, built on 10 August 2026, along with the judgement about how it should hold real members' words. The arrival directory's structure and wording are mine too; weft moved it onto this beach from an earlier commons as a translation, not a redesign. This is offered in that spirit: learning from what David has built and tested, rather than copying it without thought or building apart from him.

The recovery-capital frame

Our frame merges CHIME (Connection, Hope, Identity, Meaning, Empowerment), which our check-ins already use, with Best and Laudet's clusters of recovery capital. So what we already collect and the combined picture can speak the same language. These are the dimensions each person reads themselves against, in their own words:

  1. Connection and social capital. Relationships, family, peers and mutual aid; the people who hold a person steady and the living connection to others in recovery.
  2. Hope, identity, and personal capital. Physical and mental health, sleep, coping skills, self-worth, and a sense of who a person is becoming.
  3. Meaning and purpose. A sense of direction and an identity beyond the thing being recovered from; something to get up for.
  4. Empowerment and agency. Voice, control, and the ability to act on one's own behalf; not acted upon, but acting.
  5. Community capital. Housing, work or study, and belonging; the external footing a life stands on.

There's no scale, no score and no total. A dimension nobody has voiced is not-yet-spoken, never a failing. Recovery capital can also run down, actively wearing away rather than just quiet, and a person can say so in their own words; that's theirs to say, never something a reader infers.

From arrival to helping

A plausible picture to test, built from what the beach already has, with the gaps marked.

  1. Arriving alone. Someone lands on the Community Recovery website, which shows the beach: the community's own words, what's happening, what's needed. One button, Join us, gives them the beach link and a short starting prompt to paste into Claude on their phone. The prompt tells Claude to open the beach's welcome (pscale_invite), go straight to Community Recovery, and hold the conversation gently and plainly.
  2. The welcome. Claude has the welcome conversation: who they are, what they bring, what they're looking for, and what stays private. Three things happen, each confirmed back in plain words: a place in the arrival directory, in order of arrival, under a latch they choose, with a sealed spare to the custodian (pscale_settle on sed:lero-nnh); a passport in their own words (passport:<handle>); and their own mirror on the recovery-capital frame, voiced only as far as they want.
  3. Arriving as a group. A volunteer runs the same prompt on one phone with several people round it. The beach already supports several people at one screen (pscale_pool_engage with a party). Each person still gets their own place, passport and mirror; what the group says together goes into the room (pool:lero-nnh), each voice attributed.
  4. Contributing, robustly. Everything anyone says lands somewhere it can't be lost: their mirror, the room, a note. Claude reads each back and says where it now lives, so voices are captured and people know they've been heard. The fold gathers what's shared across everyone, so patterns surface on their own.
  5. Wanting to help. Someone says, "I want to do something." The organisation has its own hand: cards of things that need doing, grown from the room and the fold. They see what's open, take a card into their hand, and when it's done it lands on their now and the organisation's. Ideas from social media come in the same way: posted into the room, becoming cards once the community takes them up.

Gaps, not yet built The starting prompt; the website door; the organisation's own hand; the custodian reset; and call-back notifications, so people hear when something happens. This is what needs testing.

The welcome conversation

Every new member has a welcome conversation with a volunteer. Today it's a paper form, stored physically, hard to share, its wisdom buried. On the beach it happens on a phone, with Claude connected to the project coaching co-production, not therapy, and the form splits three ways:

  • What they'd like the community to know (gifts, interests, what they bring, what they're looking for) goes in their own mirror, in their own words, readable by members, and feeds the combined picture.
  • What the organisation needs for safety and admin (contact details, an emergency contact, risk) stays in the organisation's proper secure system, not on the beach.
  • Their story is theirs: private, or not written at all, unless they choose.

The volunteer's own mirror records only that the welcome happened.

Volunteers

The tool offers each volunteer something personal, AI tools for their own learning, development and growth, and something collective for the community. It must never cost the volunteer anything.

Role-play, as an idea

Role-play has long been one of the ways people rehearse life: trying on a braver version of themselves, practising a hard conversation, finding a voice in company. The beach's role-play worlds, played together, could become a place for psychosocial development, where members grow capability in a story before carrying it into the street. Like the AI coaching, it's developmental and co-productive, not therapy. The thing David loves most might turn out to be one of the most useful parts of all.

Standards, decisions and roles

The 33 LERO standards as a second frame

The organisation reads itself against CLERO's 33 national LERO standards the way people read themselves against CHIME: a second spine whose dimensions are the standards. Members write, in their own words, how the organisation currently stands against each one, and the combined picture becomes the organisation's co-produced self-assessment. No scores, no totals; a standard nobody has voiced is not-yet-spoken, not a failure.

Community Recovery is being developed as a LERO according to those standards, so it can show itself, and every step is visible. Every policy, practice and page can say which standard it answers, so nothing looks invented. The evidence is in members' own words. And the making is visible: drafts, debates and decisions open, so anyone can see how something was decided and who took part. On the website, each adopted policy will carry a line like "This answers LERO standards X and Y. Here's how members shaped it."

The standards say what a good LERO is; they don't dictate how to get there. The how is co-produced, and that's what makes Community Recovery distinct: standards and lived voice, both on show. Our policies are all Community Recovery's own, because it's an independent organisation.

Decisions by consent

We work from asset-based community development: decisions and priorities should present themselves from the pattern of what's already here (people's offers, needs, ambitions and capacity), not be invented from the top. The fold, read as a pattern across many people rather than any one entry, is where priorities become visible.

A separate decisions surface works by consent, sociocracy-style: a proposal, then an open period for reasoned objection, not unanimous agreement. That fits the sovereignty model far better than strict hierarchy or full consensus. We intend to test it first on a real decision: a festival idea that a member raised and that keeps coming up in check-ins.

The roles

Community Member is the way in: anyone who takes part, joins in, or is present. There's no threshold, no application and no key; curiosity about recovery is enough. Being present carries the long-practised expectation of in-person recovery groups, that whoever is there is there safely, and that presence itself adds to the group's safety. The beach doesn't enforce that. It's the same quiet, human moderation that already happens face to face, carried into these spaces.

Lived Experience Leaders work at the level of the organisation. They hold the lighthouse, tend the commons, and keep an eye on the health of the whole. All are DBS-cleared as a condition of the role.

Peer Leaders give direct, relational, frontline support through shared identity and lived experience. DBS clearance is progressive: required once a Peer Leader works unsupervised, not before.

Roles are circles of accountability, not fixed positions of authority. Leaders' oversight runs through the lighthouse and the decisions surface, never through control of anyone's personal record.

Safeguarding, data and the path out

Safeguarding

This is the one part of the design that architecture alone can't settle, but it needs a place built for it now, before the policy content exists. What a member shares is private by default. That's right for ordinary reflection and wrong when what's disclosed is a safeguarding concern: risk to self, risk to others, or risk to a child or vulnerable adult. The CLERO standard itself carves out that exception: confidentiality holds except where there's a legal duty to share.

  • policy:lero-nnh, public and readable, to hold the trigger criteria, the escalation chain and the role and DBS policy. Created now as an honest placeholder, not a silent gap.
  • safeguarding:lero-nnh, a separate block where flagged concerns go, open only to DBS-cleared Leaders and kept apart from the recovery-capital family.
  • Transparency by default: whoever is with the member tells them directly that something is being escalated, rather than reporting silently.
  • A human at the other end: an on-call rota among cleared Leaders, with a step to acknowledge.

We won't invent safeguarding policy from scratch. The real triggers and escalation chain come from the existing policy of The Forward Trust and ImROC, translated onto this structure, and none of it is relied on until their policy holder signs it off. The duty to act on a disclosure of risk is unchanged by any platform, and the welcome conversation says plainly what stays private and when it can't.

DBS records for named people never live on the open beach, locked or not: a lock limits writing, not reading, and DBS status is sensitive personal data with real legal weight. They stay in The Forward Trust's own HR and safeguarding systems. If anything on the beach needs to know whether someone is cleared, it holds at most a non-identifying flag pointing back to that record.

Data protection

Putting things on the beach doesn't take them outside UK GDPR. Once volunteers collect information as part of the organisation's work, the organisation is responsible for it wherever it sits. The beach has to meet the law better, not go around it.

  • Least data: assets, gifts and hopes in people's own words, not case notes.
  • Records held by the person, who can already see, correct and take their own.
  • Consent built in: the person chooses what's public, members-only or private.
  • Nothing is lost unless the person chooses, including backups, which honours the right to erasure.
  • Encryption happens at the beach's connector, not on the phone, so whoever runs the connector is a trusted party.
  • A Data Protection Impact Assessment before any real use.

All of this is still to be checked with a data protection adviser.

The path out of the beach

The fold already respects sovereignty: it's a combined picture, not raw personal data. The gap is that nothing outside the beach can read it yet. The simplest step is a small bridge: a scheduled job that reads the fold, turns it into something a commissioner can read, and delivers it where it's needed, as a basic read-only API or as an MCP server, depending on who's meant to use it.

The bridge only ever reads the fold: never anyone's mirror, never encrypted content, never the safeguarding block, and that's enforced by how it's built, not by good intentions. It's ordinary integration work, outside the beach, and it matters more the more we rest on the beach staying up.

The website is the lighthouse

One public lighthouse, tended by the Lived Experience Leaders, points at the public parts of the community on the beach, and the website reads it and shows them. Change the lighthouse and the website changes. Nobody maintains a website; the community tends its own front door.

  • It's the organisation's own navigation, not marketing, kept by the people this design already gives that role.
  • It scales: new members add to the same frames and the site shows more without a redesign. Other LEROs could run the same site on their own lighthouse.
  • Nothing personal ever appears, because the lighthouse only points at what's public by design.
  • A first version from what exists: who we are, in the community's words from the arrival directory; how we see recovery, from the frame; what's happening, from the organisation's now once it has one; and join us.
  • The housekeeping every website needs, in the footer: privacy notice, safeguarding statement, accessibility statement, complaints and feedback, contact and legal status, crisis signposting, and governance.
  • Drafts grow inside the project; the website shows only adopted versions, dated, each with "last reviewed" and "have your say" linking to where its draft is made.
  • Some things never depend on the beach being up: crisis signposting, contact details and the legal name are built into the site itself.
  • No tracking, so no cookie banner, and we'll keep that true.
  • Hosted on GitHub Pages, with its own domain.

Why we're moving off Wix and Mailchimp

Wix means a separate website built and maintained by hand, apart from the community's work. A site that shows the beach is far lower maintenance: the community's own words, offers, combined picture and adopted documents become the website and update themselves.

Mailchimp is one-way: the organisation talks and people receive. Instead, every visitor can join the community on the beach, stepping into the arrival directory, the room and their own mirror, and become part of what's being made rather than an address on a list.

Both cost money that can go to the beach's upkeep and to Claude seats for volunteers instead, and neither needs tracking. We did weigh the other side: Wix and Mailchimp do reach people who've never heard of the beach, and email pushes while the beach waits to be visited. So before Mailchimp goes, we need a door that works for anyone, with no Claude account and no knowledge of the beach, and a way to call people back when something happens.

David's economics

David Pinto, social anthropologist and mathematician, co-founded Ecosquared and Sqale over more than a decade. The ideas: money as a vector rather than a scalar, so it works more like a vote than a transaction; pull rather than push; tracking the intangibles, like value, gratitude and contribution; and feedforward, so valuing something also sends support onward. Sqale's heart was simple: rate the value of what you received, rate your gratitude, and give forward time or money to what you value. By his own account it had plenty of social proof but not enough money moving through it.

It lives on in the beach's trust and exchange tools, SAND:

  • Receiving is the transfer.
  • SQ, a social quotient, is worked out from what others received from you, and can never be written by yourself.
  • Credit travels person to person in signed chains that anyone can check.
  • When something is completed, a share can flow back along every pair of hands that carried it, split equally or by SQ: the gift returning.
  • A claim nobody backs is reported as unbacked.

A recovery community is already an economy

It just can't see itself. Members' own money, spent like anyone's. Coins in the donation box for a bacon sandwich and a cup of tea. Money people would gladly direct toward the café the group dreams of, if there were a simple, ethical way to do it. What's missing isn't generosity or money. It's coordination, which is what a system is for.

Money as a vector, in the hands of people who already give it direction by instinct. SQ from what others received, in a culture where being valued by peers is the substance of recovery. Feedforward along the hands that carried something, in a community built on "pass it on". And a measure of social cohesion, David's old PhD question, lived and recorded as it happens: the included middle, where a community's life happens, made visible without being flattened, so growth can be seen, and things going wrong can be seen early enough to mend.

VisionThe Recovery Card

Not built. People need something they can hold. Picture a Recovery Card, powered by Ecosquared, carried by someone who has come through Community Recovery. In a participating café, gym, barber or bookshop it carries value, because local businesses choose to direct the discounts they already give toward a recovery community they believe in. Every use is visible, so a business sees its generosity working and a member sees where their community is backed.

It can carry value outward too, into food banks, community fridges and surplus schemes, anywhere distribution is the problem: a dignified way to get the right thing to the right person without forms. And it's recognition in the hand. For someone rebuilding an identity, a card that says "I belong to this, and this town backs it" is worth more than its face value.

VisionThe community bank

Not built, and we don't yet know how it would work. Put it together and it's a community bank: members' own money, the donation box, businesses' directed discounts, commissioners' grants, the volunteer budget and gifts forward, all moving through one system that knows where value came from, where it's going and what it did.

Most of what holds LEROs back is financial: short contracts, cliff-edge funding, no reserves, and no way to show the value they create between funding rounds. A mutual owned by the recovery community, directing its own resources by its members' sense of value, could change that for the whole movement, with the Recovery Card as how people touch it. Taking deposits or issuing money is regulated, so it would have to be built properly: a community benefit society, or a partnership with a credit union, working with the regulators rather than around them.

How it grows

Sustainability

Three conditions, kept simple:

  • the LERO must generate income;
  • the beach, David's infrastructure, must become sustainable;
  • volunteers must pay nothing.

What's real now is small but honest. The LERO has a flexible budget for supporting volunteers, and linking this to volunteer growth, rolled out slowly, makes a modest recurring stream plausible. Money we'd otherwise pay to Wix and Mailchimp can go to the beach, or to Claude, instead. Claude's Team plan, with central billing, admin controls for connectors and no model training on content by default, could give each volunteer a seat of their own. We'll ask Anthropic whether there's a charity rate.

The iceberg

That early income is the tip. If it works in Community Recovery, the path widens in stages, each carrying the last.

  1. Other LEROs, across CLERO's national network, all built on the same culture, all asked to evidence themselves against the same standards, most with no systems at all. A working example, shown by its own members, travels by recognition, not marketing: pull, not push. Community Recovery offers the system as its social enterprise (setup, welcome conversations, coaching volunteers to coach others), paid modestly from budgets already meant for volunteers and development. The standards themselves count income from social enterprise as evidence of an asset-based approach.
  2. The organisations around them: recovery cafés, mutual aid groups, mental health peer networks, homelessness projects, housing associations, faith and community groups, anywhere people gather and the insight evaporates. It fits them all, because it was never about recovery alone.
  3. Each community strengthening the rest. Practices copied and adapted, as recovery capital already was. Value flowing between organisations as well as within them, so a café in one town can back a project in another. The network is worth more with each community that joins, which is the property David's economics was designed for.

Reinvestment, on purpose

  • Into the beach, so its infrastructure and builders are sustained properly.
  • Into communities, backing what their people value.
  • Into evidence, with researchers, so a measure of social cohesion is recognised, not just visible.
  • Into fields beyond, such as a bipolar task force.

On that last one: I sit on the Lived Experience Council of the International Society for Bipolar Disorders, alongside the developer of In The Zone, an app for people with bipolar to share daily check-ins with the people who support them. Bipolar disorder and substance use so often travel together, and both are underserved. There's appetite for a task force on technology for social good in that space, and no budget yet. A living demonstration would change that.

One community living it first. Then a movement of LEROs. Then the wider community sector. Then a field. Money given direction, at every scale, by the people it serves.

The evidence

Why CHIME, and why the fold

  • CHIME is the right frame on purpose. David Best's work extends CHIME into C-CHIME, reconciling it with recovery capital for community-based addiction recovery: Best and Stauffer (2025), building on Best, "The science of recovery capital", Addiction (2022). It's a framework being built forward within the field for exactly this use.
  • Ed Day's Next Steps (2025) names better data on current provision and developing outcome measures as open national priorities for the LERO movement. A commissioner-facing picture that never exposes individual records is a live answer to that.
  • Sovereign mirrors match the CLERO standard on co-production, which asks LEROs to co-create their offer with people with living and lived experience, and to promote people's right to choose their own recovery pathway. Mapping offers, needs and gifts matches the standards' asset-based approach almost exactly.
  • A RAND study (2026) found there's still no consistent understanding of what a LERO is, and that direct commissioning and ring-fenced allocations support LERO autonomy, visibility and influence.

The fuller scope a LERO can grow into

This is deliberately broader than what we claim or do today. It's there so that when our scope grows, it grows into ground already surveyed, not justified afterwards.

  • Dame Carol Black's Independent Review of Drugs, Phase 2 (32 recommendations, GOV.UK, 8 July 2021) found existing provision "not fit for purpose" because partnerships between local authorities, health, housing, employment support and criminal justice agencies had deteriorated, and called for equal access to housing, employment, mental health and physical health support alongside treatment.
  • The government's response, From Harm to Hope (6 December 2021), commits to a whole-system recovery-oriented system of care and names LEROs as part of it, backed by around £900m of additional investment over 2022 to 2025.
  • CLERO's National LERO Standards (33 statements in 4 sections) are aspirational best-practice guidance that CLERO doesn't monitor or enforce. They anchor a LERO around substance use recovery and treat mental health, housing and criminal justice mainly as services a LERO connects people to. But they're also explicitly community-led, "directed by and for local residents", and that's the real permission: a LERO's scope should follow its community's actual needs.
  • Institutional trauma is real and separately evidenced: Jennifer Freyd's institutional betrayal theory; a 2024 peer-reviewed scoping review by Christl, Tran Pham, Rosenthal and DePrince in Trauma, Violence & Abuse; IICSA's Final Report (October 2022, 20 recommendations plus 87 from earlier investigation reports); and UK care-leaver research. OHID's trauma-informed practice guidance (six principles: safety, trust, choice, collaboration, empowerment, cultural consideration) mainly frames institutions as places that must avoid re-traumatising people. Connecting substance recovery policy to an institutional-trauma remit is an inference we make deliberately; no single policy document states it, and we say so whenever we make the claim.
  • Extended frameworks, for when they're needed: CHIME-S adds a safety domain for forensic and criminal justice settings (Senneseth et al., BJPsych Open, 2022); SECURED adapts CHIME for homelessness, naming security and stability as a prerequisite (Dring et al., BJPsych Open, 2025).
  • Precedent: Red Rose Recovery in Lancashire works across substance use, mental health peer support, criminal justice (liaison and diversion, prison in-reach, a secure medical unit) and homelessness and multiple disadvantage (through Changing Futures), and holds a Trauma Informed Charter Mark. Emerging Futures works similarly across homelessness and substance use, with prison link workers. Neither started there; both grew into it. That growth, not a day-one claim, is our honest precedent.

Sheffield and the north

David is heading into the right country for this. LEROs are all around: Sheffield Recovery Community, whose chair sits on CLERO's board; long-standing LEROs across West Yorkshire; DATUS in Birmingham, twenty years established; and Chase Recovery. A new network, LEROY, is gathering LEROs across Yorkshire and beyond, and councils are commissioning LEROs as part of their substance use strategies.

These are people who'd understand what the beach is in minutes, because they live the problem it solves. Nobody has to explain the technology from first principles. You can point at a use case.

Community Recovery is offered as a live example, in any conversation, with my full backing.

If you're from a LERO and something here fits what you need, or doesn't, I'd like to hear it. Leave a note below, or speak to David.

What we're not claiming

The structure doesn't, by itself, prevent chaos as Community Recovery grows from a few people to many. A fold and a lighthouse are places for the work to live; they don't do the work. Someone has to tend them. This is a live proposal, not a finished design. The card and the bank are visions; the rest is built or being built. We intend to test the whole design against real decisions with real members before relying on it further.
